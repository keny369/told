Why it usually doesn't happen

It doesn't get started.

1

There's time

You mean to. After Christmas, when work calms down, when the kids are back at school, when Dad's in a better mood, when Mom's feeling stronger. There's always a reason it's not this week. Then one day there isn't a next week, and what you didn't ask went with them.

2

It can be awkward

You know the stories they always tell. It's the ones they don't that you also want. What you were like as a child. How they met. What their own parents were like, and theirs. The brother or aunt you know existed but can't quite place. You don't quite know how to ask, or whether you should, and with everything else going on it can feel selfish to. So it waits. Sitting them down with a phone and saying "tell me about your life" isn't how anyone talks.

Told takes the asking off you. You tell us who it's for. We send a link to their phone. From then on the app asks, they talk, and the story builds itself in their own words. It asks the way a good listener does, so the stories you've never heard come out alongside the ones you have, and you don't have to be the one asking. Today those stories live in their head, and only there. Afterwards they're written down, recorded, printed and kept, in their voice, for your children to hear one day.

How it works

Six conversations, at whatever pace suits them. Then a book. Ten minutes to set up, a few minutes after each conversation, one read-through at the end. At about one conversation a week, the finished story is ready in around two months, and the book arrives a few weeks after you say yes.

1

Set it up in ten minutes

Tell us who the story is about and what you call them. We send a link to their phone; tapping it opens the app, ready to go. Add a few names you know will come up, if you like.

2

They tell it

Told opens by asking for the story they've told many times, the one the family could tell for them, so the first conversation feels like a chat, not an interview. Then it follows their life the way they remember it and asks for the moments behind the facts: not "where did you work" but "what happened that day". Six conversations in all, usually twenty to thirty minutes each, whenever suits them, and never cut short. At the end it checks the few names it wasn't sure of, and they just say yes or no.

A few minutes from you. After each conversation, an email with the handful of names and places we couldn't settle, each with a few seconds of them saying it. It never holds anything up. The first chapter is waiting for you after the first conversation, so you know what you're getting before the second.

Their story, in their words, in chapters, told the way they told it. Tap anything and hear them say it. Nothing is smoothed, invented or improved: if they said "I think it was 1968", that's what it says, and anything we can't tie to their voice is checked by a person, not guessed.

Every reason this usually fails

And what we do about each one

They won't do it

They don't have to do anything but talk. Told asks, they answer: one question at a time, big type, one button, and a link on their phone that opens it ready to go. No one in the family has to nag.

They ramble

Good. Rambling is where the stories are. Told follows them, asks for the moment behind the thing they just mentioned, and keeps all of it; what doesn't fit a chapter gets its own place at the end. Nothing is ever cut short, and if they want to talk longer or sooner, they do. The app reminds them when the next conversation is due, so you don't have to.

There's something they don't want to talk about

They say so and the app moves on. It's never asked again. The hard parts come last, once they're used to it, and they can stop any time.

They say something private

They decide who can hear it, from their phone, and can change their mind. They can leave anything out, and they can delete everything. The family manages the story within what they've allowed, not beyond it. They also choose what happens to it after they're gone.

Something happens to them halfway

Everything recorded so far is kept, in full, within what they allowed. We build the story from what exists, and the book can still be printed.

They get tired and need to stop

Then they stop. Everything they said is kept, and the next conversation picks up where they left off. A conversation that ends early doesn't count as one of the six: short sittings add up, so they always get six full conversations.

What phone do they need?

An iPhone from 2018 or newer (iPhone XR, XS or later) on iOS 17 or later, with Wi-Fi at home and a little free space. They don't need to know their way around apps; the link opens it ready to go. Android is coming. If they use Android, tell us when you get in touch.

Do they have to speak English?

For now, the conversations are in English. Names, places and words from another language are kept exactly as they said them, and if you tell us which languages are spoken at home, we'll spell them right. Conversations in other languages come later.

It sounds like a machine wrote it

It's their words, in their order, and you can hear them say every one. We don't polish, because polish is where people stop sounding like themselves. Anything we can't tie to their voice is checked by a person, not smoothed over, and you read the whole thing before we print.

We lose it

You can't. Every recording is kept in full and backed up, and you can download all of it whenever you like, in a form that works without us. If Told disappeared tomorrow, their voice wouldn't. The story page is included for five years; after that, a small yearly fee, or simply keep your copy.

Can six conversations hold a whole life?

No, and we don't pretend they can. What you get is a portrait: the shape of their life in the chapters they chose, the scenes told properly, their people, their sayings, and what they wanted kept. The book says which chapters were covered and lists the ones left open at the back, so you know. Shorter packages, one story about their parents, one trip, one lesson, come later.
